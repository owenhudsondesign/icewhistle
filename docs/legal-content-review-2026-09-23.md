# Know-Your-Rights content: items needing legal review

**Prepared 23 September 2026.** Findings from automated web research, not legal
advice. Every item below needs review by a qualified immigration attorney
before any of it goes into the app. Nothing here has been applied to the app's
guidance — the content in `src/data/knowledge-base.ts` and the rights pages is
unchanged.

Why this file exists: the app's legal guidance was checked against sources
dated after its content was written. Several things appear to have moved. The
app currently carries no "last reviewed" date, so there is no way for a reader
to tell how current the advice is.

---

## The app's current guidance appears to still be correct

These were checked and no contradicting authority was found:

- Right to remain silent
- Right to speak to a lawyer before answering questions
- Do not sign anything without speaking to an attorney
- Do not lie or provide false documents
- Ask "Am I free to go?"
- Do not open the door without a judicial warrant signed by a judge; an ICE
  administrative warrant (Form I-200 / I-205) does not authorise entry
- Right to record enforcement activity in public

## Gaps and caveats flagged for review

Listed most-consequential first. Each needs independent verification.

### 1. Expedited removal — possible major gap

Research indicates the expansion of expedited removal beyond border areas was
upheld on appeal in June 2026, meaning someone who cannot show roughly two
years of continuous US presence could be removed quickly without seeing a
judge. If that is accurate, the practical advice — keep documents proving two
or more years of continuous presence, and keep them somewhere reachable — is
currently missing from the app entirely.

Source to verify: <https://www.migrationpolicy.org/article/trump-expedited-removal>

### 2. Administrative warrants and forced home entry — contested

The app's advice (judicial warrant only) appears to remain the correct legal
position to assert. However, research indicates DHS has an internal policy
claiming I-205 warrants justify forced entry for people with final removal
orders, that agents have acted on it, and that at least two federal district
courts have ruled against the government. If so, the advice is right but
incomplete: a reader should understand the position is contested and weigh
personal safety alongside asserting the right.

Sources to verify:
<https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/dhs-warrantless-home-entry-memos-fourth-amendment-problem>
<https://www.justsecurity.org/130497/dhs-warrantless-home-entry-fourth-amendment/>

### 3. Pressure to sign voluntary departure — possible gap

"Do not sign anything" is generic. Research indicates a sharp rise in people
signing voluntary departure or stipulated removal under pressure in detention,
which waives the right to a hearing. A concrete, named warning may be more
useful than the general advice.

Source to verify:
<https://nipnlg.org/sites/default/files/2026-05/Voluntary-Departure-Explainer.pdf>

### 4. Right to record — possible missing limit

The general right to record enforcement activity in public appears intact.
Research indicates a 2026 criminal conviction for following an agent to their
home and livestreaming, which suggests the right does not extend to trailing
an officer to a private residence or publicising a home address. Worth
confirming and, if accurate, stating the boundary.

### 5. Courthouse arrests — possible favourable change

Research indicates a June 2026 ruling vacated the courthouse-arrest policy
nationwide and reinstated a 12-hour cap on short-term holding. Recent rulings
can be appealed or stayed, so current status should be confirmed before
anything is said about it in the app.

### 6. Jurisdictional variation

Detainer compliance, agent mask/ID requirements, and courthouse protections
appear to vary considerably by state and circuit, and to be actively
litigated. The app presents guidance nationally. Consider whether it should
say plainly that protections differ by state.

## Recommended regardless of the above

1. **Add a "last reviewed" date** to the rights content, so a reader can judge
   how current it is.
2. **Link to a maintained authority** — for example
   <https://www.aclu.org/know-your-rights/immigrants-rights> — so someone can
   check the present position when this app's content has aged.
3. **Set a review cadence.** Several of these items moved within months.

## Method and limits

Findings come from automated web search and page fetches on 23 September 2026,
not from primary legal research. Some claims were corroborated only by
secondary reporting. Treat every item as a lead to verify, not a conclusion.
No item here has been written into the app.
